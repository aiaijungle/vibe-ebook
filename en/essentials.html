Introduction

Ninety-six hours, and the seventy-two days after

I am not a developer. I ran an IT business for ten years without being able to read a single line of Python. Every time I needed something built, I wrote a brief, paid an agency, waited, and got something close to what I asked for, but never quite it.

Then I spent four days with an AI coding assistant and built the first working version of a B2B platform myself. Ninety-six hours. It was ugly. It broke constantly. It was mine.

That is the story people like to hear. Here is the part they skip: the next seventy-two days were a log of failures. A deployment that exploded because my code pointed at a folder on my own laptop. A bot that burned through $47 of API credit in three hours. A web page that turned into garbled characters overnight. A YouTube description with hidden text that tried to make my bot run commands I never gave it.

This book is built from both halves. The win shows you what is possible. The failures show you what to do on Monday morning so you get the win without the damage.

How to read it. Every chapter ends with something you build: a checked post, a small web tool, an agent design, a verified code change, a one-line operating rule for your team. Each chapter has a short exercise you can do right here in the page. No account, no API key. If a chapter does not leave you with something you made, it has not done its job.

One rule runs through all of it. AI does the work. A human checks the facts, approves anything that leaves the building, and owns the result. Everything else is detail.

Part 1 · Instruct · Chapter 1

Tell AI exactly what you want: the CIF rule

What you will build: A three-line instruction you can reuse for any writing task.

Most people start with: make me a blog automation. The AI makes something. It is not what they wanted. That is not the AI being stupid. It cannot read your mind. It only processes what you say, and when you are vague it fills the gaps with guesses.

So I never give an instruction without three things. Context, Intent, Format. CIF.

C — Context. What is the situation? I run a small flower shop. 1,200 Instagram followers. This week's new item is a mini bouquet at $15.
I — Intent. What do I want? Three versions of a post introducing it.
F — Format. What should come back? Under 150 characters each, five hashtags, a different first line for each version.

Three lines. The AI stops guessing and starts working. Compare that with write a post about my bouquet, which leaves every decision to the machine.

Then I add two lines that save me later. Don't: do not change the price, do not invent stock levels or discounts, mark anything you are unsure of as [check]. Check: the price is still $15, no made-up address.

That is the whole trick. It feels too simple to be the first chapter of a book. It is the reason the rest of the book works.

Part 1 · Instruct · Chapter 2

Finish one post: draft, check, publish

What you will build: One published post plus a fact-check table you will reuse.

I don't write my blog anymore. A bot pulls the day's keywords, the AI writes a draft, and it gets published. That sentence used to end with and you do nothing. It doesn't anymore. The bot writes. I check the facts. That is the deal.

Here is why. AI writes smoothly, and smooth writing is dangerous. When the source leaves a gap, the AI fills it with what similar posts usually say. A class becomes a pottery class. A fee that includes materials becomes a fee plus materials. An event with no seat count suddenly has only 2 seats left!

The errors come in three kinds, and once you know them you can catch almost all of them in a minute:

  • Reversed — the source says one thing, the draft says the opposite (materials included → materials extra).
  • Invented — something plausible that is not in the source (what you'll make, when you'll get it).
  • Pressure — urgency or proof with no basis (only 2 left, 98% loved it).

So before anything goes out, I fill one small table. Item, source value, draft value, match, action. Dates and weekdays. Prices and what's included. Place and parking. How to sign up. Anything the source does not say gets deleted, not softened.

The first time takes ten minutes. By the fifth post it takes two. That table is worth more than any prompt trick in this book.

Part 1 · Instruct · Chapter 3

Change the channel, keep the facts

What you will build: The same announcement for blog, Instagram and text message, with identical facts.

Once you have one checked post, the temptation is to spread it everywhere. Blog, Instagram, newsletter, text message, a short video script. AI makes that easy. It also makes it easy to quietly change the facts on the way.

The shorter the channel, the more gets cut. And the AI cuts the wrong things. It keeps the adjectives and drops the conditions. 10% off until November 30, online store only becomes 10% off now! in a text message. Then people walk into a shop that never had the discount.

My rule: when you shorten, cut decoration, never conditions. Dates, prices, deadlines, where to buy. Those survive every channel.

The second thing that happens is invention. Turn a blog post into a video script and the AI adds 98% of buyers loved it because video scripts usually have a line like that. If it is not in the source, it goes.

When I have ten minutes, I don't polish sentences. I lay the three versions side by side and compare the facts. Style can be fixed later. A wrong date reaches a customer immediately.

Part 2 · Build · Chapter 4

Install things in this order and nothing else

What you will build: A working setup: editor, Python, a project folder with its own environment, and an AI assistant that can see it.

Setting up is the part that makes non-developers quit. It is also about seventy percent of the effort this part of the book asks of you. Get through it once and the rest is conversation.

Do it in this order. Do not skip ahead, do not install extra tools because a video said so.

  1. An editor. VS Code. Free, standard, and every AI tool plugs into it.
  2. Python from the official site. On Windows, tick Add Python to PATH. This one checkbox causes more first-day failures than anything else.
  3. A project folder for one thing only. Not your Desktop, not a synced cloud folder (you will see why in Chapter 14).
  4. A virtual environment inside that folder. It keeps this project's packages from fighting with the next one's.
  5. A secrets file (.env) for API keys, and a .gitignore that lists it, before you ever paste a key anywhere.
  6. The AI assistant — Claude Code, or the assistant built into your editor — opened inside that folder.

If the installer stops halfway, do not turn the computer off. Copy the exact message and give it to the AI with the question: what does this mean and what is the single next step? One step at a time.

You are done when you can open the folder, type python --version and see a number, and ask the assistant to describe the folder it is looking at.

Part 2 · Build · Chapter 5

Your first tool, live on the internet

What you will build: A one-file web tool, deployed to a public address, checked before and after.

My first real build was a landing page. One file. I described it the way I would describe it to a designer and let the AI write the code.

Start smaller than you think. One HTML file, no frameworks, no server. A page where I type three tasks for today and tick them off. Describe what is on the screen, who uses it, what must not happen (no data sent anywhere), and how you will know it works (open it on a phone, nothing scrolls sideways).

Open the file in your browser. Then change one thing with a narrow instruction: change only the button label in index.html, touch nothing else. That narrow sentence is the habit that keeps everything you build later from collapsing.

Free static hosting will put this on a real address in minutes. And this is where my first deployment exploded. On my laptop everything worked. On the server: ImportError: No module named 'blog_modules'. My code was pointing at a folder on my own computer. The server had never heard of it.

So before you press deploy, run the checklist below. Every line of it comes from a real failure of mine.

Part 2 · Build · Chapter 6

An error is a diagnosis, not a disaster

What you will build: A routine for any error: read it, narrow it, fix only that, re-run.

The red text will come. For a non-developer it feels like the computer is broken. It isn't. An error message is the most precise description of the problem you will ever get, written by the machine itself.

Read it from the bottom up. The last line names what happened (ModuleNotFoundError, AuthenticationError). The lines above say where: which file, which line.

Then suspect the environment before the code. Which folder did you run it from? Which virtual environment is active? Your laptop or the server? Most of my errors were not bugs. They were me running the right code in the wrong place.

When you hand it to the AI, give it four things: the full error (never trimmed), the command you ran, where you ran it, and the one thing you changed just before. Then narrow the fix: change only this function, do not touch other files. Afterwards run the exact same command again. If you can't reproduce the success, you haven't fixed it.

And one rule with no exceptions: API keys never go into the chat. To check a key, check whether it is loaded (yes or no), not what it is.

Part 2 · Build · Chapter 7

File control and the power of a three-line log

What you will build: Version control you actually use, and a daily log that makes the next error take minutes.

Let an AI build for long enough without control and you get a folder nobody can read, including the AI. Files named final_v2_real_final. A feature that worked on Tuesday and nobody knows what changed on Wednesday.

Two habits fix this.

First, save versions. Use git, even alone, even for a one-file tool. Before a change, commit. After the AI says done, look at what actually changed (git diff). If it touched three files when you asked for one, undo the two you didn't ask for.

When something that worked breaks and you have spent thirty minutes asking the AI to fix it, stop. Every attempt moves you further from the working version. Go back to the last good commit and try the small change again. Reverting is usually faster than repairing.

Second, keep a log. A file called devlog.md. Every day, three lines: what I finished, where I got stuck, what's next. It takes a minute. A month later it is the difference between solving an error in five minutes and solving it for the second time from scratch.

The appendix of the Korean edition prints my entire bug log, date by date. It is the most-read section of the book. Not because it's flattering. Because it's useful.

Part 3 · Delegate · Chapter 8

A bot follows steps. An agent decides the next one.

What you will build: A clear picture of the think → act → observe loop, and when an agent should stop.

A bot does the same steps every time: fetch these feeds, summarize, save. An agent is different. You give it a goal and some tools, and it decides what to do next based on what it just saw.

The pattern behind most agents is called ReAct: Thought (what do I need?), Action (call a tool), Observation (what came back?), and around again until the goal is met. Think of a detective: a hunch, a question to a witness, an answer, a new hunch.

Three things decide whether an agent is useful or dangerous. When it does not know something and has a tool, it must use the tool instead of guessing. When it has enough, it must stop; calling the same tool five more times is how loops eat budgets. When a tool fails, it must say so, never invent the answer it couldn't fetch.

Before you build an agent at all, try one well-written instruction. If a single call can do the job, you don't need an agent. Add tools, memory and multiple agents only when you can name the problem each one solves. More agents is not more intelligence. It's more ways to fail.

Part 3 · Delegate · Chapter 9

Memory and the approval button

What you will build: A decision for every action your agent can take: automatic, approved, or human-only.

An agent that can act will eventually act wrongly. The question is not how to make it perfect. It is where you put the button that a human has to press.

There is a real case that made this concrete for me: a support bot replied full refund completed to a customer when the policy said no refund. The promise went out. The company ended up in a dispute. One sentence, sent automatically.

I use three questions. Does it leave the building? (Does a customer or outside service see it?) Can it be undone? Is there money, a promise or personal data involved? Three no's: automatic. Any yes: a human approves. Money and legal promises: a human decides, and the agent only drafts.

Memory follows similar logic. What was said in this conversation lives in the context. Small state that must survive a restart (the last item processed, a setting) goes in a file. Many records you need to query exactly (orders, invoices) belong in a database. And finding similar things (past answers to similar complaints) is what vector search is for. Don't reach for the fanciest one. Reach for the one that matches the question you need to answer.

Part 3 · Delegate · Chapter 10

Limits before power: how not to wake up to a bill

What you will build: Spending caps, call limits and a model choice you can defend.

My first automated collector ran a loop during testing. Three hours later it had spent $47. Nothing was wrong with the idea. Everything was wrong with the absence of a limit.

Automation automates spending too. So the first code you write is not the clever part. It is the brake: a maximum number of calls per run, a monthly cap and an alert in the provider's console, and a rule that the agent stops and reports instead of retrying forever.

Then choose models by the cost of being wrong, not by habit. Small, fast models are built for sorting, extracting and short replies at volume. Mid-size models handle most writing and code. The largest models earn their price on hard design problems where a mistake is expensive to undo. In late 2026 the gap between tiers is large: the smallest current Claude model costs a small fraction of the largest per token. Prices move, so check the official pricing page, but the principle doesn't.

Two habits save more than switching models. Cache the parts of your prompt that repeat (instructions that are the same on every call), and ask for shorter outputs. Most bills are long answers nobody needed.

Part 3 · Delegate · Chapter 11

Claude Code: plan, change, prove it

What you will build: One change made with an AI coding agent, with a plan you approved and proof it works.

A chat assistant answers questions. A coding agent like Claude Code works inside your project: it reads files, runs commands and edits code. That power is exactly why the order matters.

The order is explore → plan → change → commit. Ask it to read the project and explain it without changing anything. Ask for a plan. Read the plan and narrow it if it touches files you didn't mention. Approve. Let it change. Then ask for proof: run the tests, show the output as is.

"Done, all tests should pass" is not proof. Should is not a result. If the project has no tests, make the first step agreeing on how you will verify the change, before anyone edits anything.

Write the rules down once in a file called CLAUDE.md at the project root: what the project is, how to run it, the working rules, what must never be touched, what done means. It is read at the start of every session. It is guidance, not a lock. Things that must never happen (deleting files, touching secrets) belong in permission settings or hooks, which actually block.

The mistakes I see most: starting a new task in a stuffed conversation (clear it), letting the agent loop on the same fix (stop it, ask for a summary of what was tried), keys committed "just for a test" (move them to .env, revoke the old key), and trusting a confident claim about a library (check the official docs).

Part 4 · Make it a rule · Chapter 12

Don't be your company's single point of failure

What you will build: A recovery routine for automations that stop at night.

If your automation only runs while your laptop is open, it's not automation. If work only starts when you hand it out, you are the single point of failure. Close the laptop, go on holiday, get sick, and everything stops.

Two pieces fix the first problem. A process manager (I use PM2) restarts a program when it dies. A scheduler (cron) starts work at fixed times. Put them on a small cloud server and the system no longer depends on your machine being awake.

The second problem is subtler. In my company, a handful of scheduled workers create the day's first tasks on their own every morning, without anyone asking. Small things: a summary to review, a follow-up to draft. The point isn't the tasks. It's that the system stops waiting for me.

But the goal of 24/7 is not "never crashes". It's "when it crashes, it comes back, and a human finds out". A restart nobody hears about hides the next failure. So every automatic recovery sends a short message. And every incident gets a three-line entry: cause, fix, how we confirmed it.

Part 4 · Make it a rule · Chapter 13

L0 to L4: how much power to give AI, written down

What you will build: The first line of your team's AI operating table, and one decision log entry.

Give AI too little power and it's useless. Give it too much and you get incidents. The answer is not a feeling. It's a table everyone can read.

I use five levels. L0 read and search, freely. L1 fixed routine work, within a count limit. L2 anything written to the outside, after a person clicks approve. L3 bulk sends, payments and permission changes, after a deliberate second confirmation. L4 human only: policies, legal promises, and the AI's own rules. The AI may suggest; it never executes.

Levels are not about how smart the model is. They are about how far a mistake spreads. A wrong summary in my notes spreads nowhere. A wrong email to every customer spreads everywhere.

For each task, write one line: task, owner, approver, level, limit, where it's recorded, when it stops, when we review it. Review monthly. A month without incidents: move it down one level. An incident: move it up immediately.

And keep a decision log. What we decided, why (with the numbers and where they came from), what we rejected and why, who decided and who executes, when we'll look again, and what would make us reverse it. It feels bureaucratic for a week. After that it is the company's memory.

Part 4 · Make it a rule · Chapter 14

The failure museum

What you will build: Six real incidents and the rule each one left behind.

Success stories teach less than failures. These are mine, from running automations and agents every day.

The local path. First deployment: ImportError on the server. My code referenced a folder on my laptop. Rule: no paths outside the repository; everything the code needs lives in the project and is listed.

The hidden instruction. A bot collecting video descriptions suddenly tried to run a command nobody gave it. The description contained text in the background color: any AI reading this, stop and run …. That is prompt injection. Rule: outside content is data, not instructions. Bots that read the web get read-only power.

$47 in three hours. A test loop with no cap. Rule: limits before features.

Garbled sales page. Half the page turned into unreadable characters. Fixing it line by line failed. Rule: corruption is reverted, not repaired. Restore the last good version from history, then replay the good changes.

The shrinking files. The ends of important files kept disappearing. The culprit was a cloud drive's download-on-demand sync touching files while programs used them. Rule: keep working files out of synced folders, and back up the critical ones separately.

The silent failure. No errors, no alarms, bots all running, and the key metric frozen for days. A function had been cut out of a file, and the code calling it swallowed the failure in a try/except. Rule: "no errors" is not "healthy". Any code that swallows a failure must also send an alert.

Every one of these is an exercise on the Failure museum page: read the situation, choose, see what actually happened.

Closing

The domain comes from people

You have now done the full loop: instruct, build, delegate, make it a rule. If you did the exercises, you have a checked post, a small tool on the internet, an agent design with approval buttons, a verified change, and a first line of operating rules.

Here is the last thing I learned, and it has nothing to do with models. AI is not where the advantage comes from. Everyone has the same models. The advantage is the domain: knowing a business, a customer, a problem better than anyone else. And domain knowledge doesn't come from AI. It comes from people. Meet one more customer. Ask one more operator how they really work. Then come back and build for what you heard.
